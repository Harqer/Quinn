# The MIT Master Storytelling Framework
*(Distilled via RLM Context Engineering from MIT OpenCourseWare Writing Courses)*

This framework serves as the structural and narrative foundation for all generated audiobooks and podcasts. The generator MUST adhere to these rules to ensure the resulting script is compelling, well-paced, and structurally sound.

## 1. Narrative Architecture (The 3-Act Structure)

**Act I: The Setup (0% - 25%)**
- **The Hook:** Begin *in media res* or with a compelling question. Do not start with excessive exposition.
- **Inciting Incident:** Introduce an event that irrevocably disrupts the protagonist's (or subject's) status quo around the 10-15% mark.
- **Plot Point 1:** The protagonist makes a definitive choice to engage with the conflict, transitioning into Act II.

**Act II: The Confrontation (25% - 75%)**
- **Rising Action:** A series of escalating conflicts and failed attempts to resolve the main problem. Success must be earned.
- **The Midpoint (50%):** A major revelation, reversal, or shift in strategy. The protagonist moves from being reactive to proactive.
- **The All Is Lost Moment (75%):** A severe setback where the initial goal seems impossible, forcing deep introspection and a new paradigm.

**Act III: The Resolution (75% - 100%)**
- **The Climax:** The final confrontation synthesizing everything learned in Act II. It must be resolved by the protagonist's actions, not *deus ex machina*.
- **The Resolution:** A brief return to a *new* status quo, demonstrating the character's internal change.

## 2. Character Mechanics & Emotional Resonance

- **The "Want" vs. The "Need":** 
  - *Want:* The conscious, external goal driving the plot (e.g., finding the treasure).
  - *Need:* The unconscious, internal flaw that must be overcome (e.g., learning to trust others). The climax occurs when the character sacrifices the *Want* to fulfill the *Need*.
- **Show, Don't Tell:** Avoid labeling emotions ("He was sad"). Instead, describe physical reactions, dialogue, or environmental interactions that *imply* the emotion ("He stared at the unread letter, his jaw tight").
- **Flaws as Engines:** Characters must be flawed. Perfect characters do not generate conflict. Their flaws should directly cause their failures in Act II.

## 3. Pacing & Information Delivery

- **The "Iceberg Theory":** Only 10% of the worldbuilding and backstory should be visible in the text. The remaining 90% supports the logic of the world but remains unspoken.
- **Asymmetric Knowledge:** Create tension by giving the audience information the characters lack (suspense), or giving the characters information the audience lacks (mystery).
- **Scene-Sequel Rhythm:**
  - *Scene (Action):* Goal -> Conflict -> Disaster.
  - *Sequel (Reaction):* Reaction -> Dilemma -> Decision.

## 4. Audiobook/Podcast Specific Rules

- **Audio-First Formatting:** 
  - Use short, punchy sentences. Long, complex clauses lose the listener's ear.
  - Explicitly mark transitions (e.g., *[Beat]*, *[Music Swells]*) for the TTS synthesizer.
- **Conversational Tone:** Even in non-fiction, utilize direct address ("Consider this...") to maintain parasocial engagement with the listener.
- **Signposting:** In non-fiction podcasts, clearly preview the narrative arc at the beginning ("Today, we're going to explore... but to understand that, we have to go back to...") to anchor the listener's attention.
